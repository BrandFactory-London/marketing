# Wet room or shower tray: which suits your bathroom?

Excerpt: Wet room vs shower tray compared on cost, installation, small-bathroom fit and upkeep, with a clear recommendation for each situation.

Wet room vs shower tray is one of the first decisions you make when planning a bathroom renovation. It shapes how the floor is built and how much work happens on site. This guide covers what each option is, where they differ on cost and installation, and which suits a small bathroom. It then compares upkeep and resale, and ends with our recommendation. ## Wet room vs shower tray: what each option is

A shower tray is a pre-formed base that sits on the floor with a drain built in. It has a raised edge, called an upstand, which holds water inside the shower area. Most trays are made from acrylic or stone resin. You add a screen, a door or a curtain to contain the spray. The tray itself is waterproof, so most of the sealing work happens where the tray edge meets the wall tiles and the floor.

A wet room works differently. There is no tray and no step. The floor is laid to a fall, a gentle slope, so that water runs towards a drain. The walls and floor are waterproofed beneath the tiles, which means the whole room can get wet without damage. Most wet rooms use either a central drain or a linear drain along one wall, with the floor falling roughly 1 in 80 towards it. A fixed glass panel usually protects the rest of the room from splashes, but a screen is optional.

The core difference is simple. A shower tray gives you a defined shower area on a raised base. A wet room turns the whole floor into part of the shower.

Shower trays come in standard sizes and work with hinged doors and sliding doors. This keeps product choice wide and makes fitting predictable. Wet rooms need more planning, because the floor build-up and the waterproofing both have to be right before the tiles go on. ## Cost and installation differences

Most wet rooms cost more than a shower tray in the same room, mainly because of the floor work. The floor often has to be built up or lowered to create the fall. A drain has to be positioned to suit that fall. The waterproofing then has to cover the whole floor and the lower sections of the walls. Each of these steps takes labour and materials that a tray does not need.

A shower tray installation is shorter. The tray is set level, the waste is connected, the joints are sealed and the enclosure is fitted. Less of the floor is disturbed, so there is less to lift, reconnect and make good afterwards.

Two structural points deserve attention before you choose a wet room.

**Floor weight.** A wet room adds weight through the screed and the tiles. The joists must carry that load, which matters most on a first floor or above another home. A structural survey will tell you whether the floor can take the build-up. If the joists need strengthening, that work adds cost and time, so allow for it early.

**Drain position.** The drain has to sit where the fall can reach it. Moving an existing waste pipe is possible, but it adds labour. A shower tray can often use the existing waste position, so ask early where a wet room drain would need to go.

**Timing.** Before any work starts, there is a pre-site phase of usually one to two weeks. Once work starts on site, a bathroom takes two to six weeks, depending on size, scope, whether the layout changes and access. A wet room usually sits toward the longer end of that range, because the waterproofing needs time to cure before the tiles go on.

## Which works in small bathrooms

Small bathrooms are where this choice matters most, because each option solves a different space problem.

A wet room can make a small room feel larger. There is no tray edge to step over and no door that swings into the room. The floor runs as one continuous surface, which reads as open. The trade-off is splashing. Water spreads across the whole floor, so the layout has to keep the toilet and basin out of the spray path. A powerful extractor fan and a drain close to the spray area both matter. A wet room works best in a small room with a simple layout, where the shower runs along one wall and the drain sits nearby.

A shower tray suits some small rooms better because it contains the water. A sliding door takes no floor space, which helps in a narrow room. A hinged door needs clear floor space to swing open, so it can block the basin or the toilet when it is open. A corner tray or a recessed tray in an alcove fits a tight footprint. An 800 mm square tray is a common size for compact bathrooms, but check the door swing against the rest of the room before you order.

Use this simple test before you decide. Mark the shower area on the floor with tape, then open and close every door through its full range. Stand where you would dry yourself and check whether the space feels usable. Ask your installer to check the drain position against the marked area.

In a narrow room with a tight footprint, a tray with a sliding door often makes the best use of space. In a small room with a simple layout and good ventilation, a wet room can look and feel more generous.

## Upkeep and resale

**Upkeep.** A wet room has more joins that need attention. The sealant or grout where the floor meets the wall, and where the walls meet the drain, should be checked from time to time for cracks or mould. Water reaches the whole floor, so squeegeeing the walls and floor after showering helps. Running the fan for a while after use keeps humidity down. Cleaning covers the whole room, not just one corner.

A shower tray keeps the wet area smaller, which makes cleaning simpler in one place. Its weak points are the seals along the tray edge and the tracks on sliding doors, where soap scum builds up. A split or mouldy seal is a small job to replace, but it needs doing before water gets behind the tray.

Both options need proper extraction. Without it, either will show condensation and staining over time.

**Resale.** Level access suits buyers who expect to stay in a home for many years, and some buyers actively look for it. A well-built wet room can be a selling point. A poorly built one is a costly problem, because water that gets past the waterproofing can go unnoticed until it causes damage. Ask your installer what written guarantee covers the waterproofing, and keep a full record of the work. We pass each building material invoice and receipt to you as it comes in, photographed and uploaded, so you have that record from the start. Shower trays are familiar to most buyers, so they rarely raise questions during a sale.

## Our recommendation

Our recommendation turns on floor depth, which decides what is possible, and on access, which decides what matters most. If the floor is too shallow to build a fall without raising the room, a wet room is not a sensible option, and a shower tray is the right choice.

We would choose a shower tray when:

- Budget is the main constraint, or you want the shorter build.
- The room is narrow and a sliding door will make better use of the space.

We would choose a wet room when:

- Step-free access matters now or may matter later, and you want the shower flush with the floor.
- The room feels cramped with a door or screen, the layout is simple and the floor is deep enough for the fall.

For a tight, budget-led bathroom, a shower tray with a sliding door is the strongest choice. For a long-term home where access and openness matter most, a wet room is worth the extra cost if the floor allows it. In every case, get a structural check and an itemised quote for both options before you commit.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a wet room always more expensive than a shower tray?

In most cases, yes. The extra cost comes from the floor build-up, the drain position and the waterproofing across the whole room. The gap depends on the size of the room and how much of the floor needs lifting. Ask for both options on your itemised quote so you can compare them directly.

### Can I have a wet room on an upper floor?

Often, yes, but the floor must be checked first. A wet room adds weight through the screed and the tiles, and the joists have to carry it. Get a structural survey before you commit to a design, and allow for any reinforcement in your budget.

### Do I need a glass screen with a wet room?

No. A wet room can have a fixed glass panel, a curtain or no screen at all. A panel helps keep water off the toilet and basin, so it is worth considering in a small room.

### How long does a wet room take compared with a shower tray?

A bathroom takes two to six weeks on site, depending on size, scope, layout changes and access. A wet room usually sits toward the longer end of that range, because of the floor work and the time the waterproofing needs before tiling. Pre-site work, which is usually one to two weeks, comes before that.
