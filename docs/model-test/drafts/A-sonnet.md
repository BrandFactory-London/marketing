# Wet room or shower tray: which suits your bathroom?

Excerpt: Wet room vs shower tray: how cost, space, upkeep and resale compare, and which one we recommend for your situation.

Wet room vs shower tray is one of the first decisions you face when you plan a bathroom renovation, and it affects almost everything that follows. It changes the floor build-up, the plumbing route, the tiling plan, the budget and how you clean the room for the next ten years. Many homeowners we meet in Chigwell, Loughton and Ilford arrive with a strong opinion based on a photo, not on the room in front of them.

This guide compares the two on the points that matter: what each one is, what it costs and how it is installed, how each behaves in a small bathroom, and what upkeep and resale look like. At the end we give a plain recommendation for each type of home.

## What is a wet room, and what is a shower tray?

A shower tray is a pre-formed base that sits on the bathroom floor. It is usually made from acrylic, stone resin or a similar material. Water lands on the tray, flows to a waste outlet and drains away through a trap underneath. A glass door or screen stops water reaching the rest of the room. The tray is a separate unit, so the rest of the bathroom floor stays dry and can be finished in any material you like.

A wet room has no tray. The whole floor, and the walls around the shower area, are waterproofed, a process known as tanking. The floor slopes gently towards a drain, either a central drain or a linear channel drain along one wall. You walk straight into the shower area at floor level. A fixed glass panel often keeps most of the spray contained, though some wet rooms have no screen at all.

The names cause some confusion. A walk-in shower with a low-profile tray is still a tray shower. A true wet room has a waterproofed floor and a fall to the drain. Check that every supplier means the same thing before you compare quotes.

## Cost and installation differences

Neither option is automatically cheaper. They put the money in different places.

### Where the money goes with a shower tray

A tray shower is the more familiar job. The tray is a bought item with a known price. The fitter sets it on a stable base, connects the waste and trap, seals the edges and tiles the walls above it. The enclosure is a separate cost, and a quality glass door with a good seal is a real line in the budget.

### Where the money goes with a wet room

A wet room costs more in labour and preparation than the shower itself. The floor must slope, so the fitter either builds up the floor with a pre-formed tile-ready base or sets the fall into a screed. The waterproofing must be continuous across the floor and up the walls, with the joins and pipe penetrations treated carefully. Then the whole area is tiled, and every extra tile means extra grout and extra cutting where the floor falls to the drain.

The drain and its position also matter. A linear drain looks tidy and lets you use large floor tiles, but it needs the fall to run in one direction. A central drain needs the fall to run from all four sides, which means more cutting and a more complicated floor.

### The floor you are starting from

This is where wet rooms either work or become expensive. On a concrete ground floor, the drain can sit in or just below the slab and the fall is easier to create. On a timber upper floor, the waste pipe has to run between the joists with enough drop to drain properly. The floor may also need strengthening so it stays rigid under tiles and does not flex and crack the waterproofing. If the pipe route does not work, a wet room upstairs can need joist changes that a shower tray would avoid.

This is why we look at the structure before we talk about style. When a customer wants a wet room and the floor does not suit it, we say so at the consultation, not halfway through the job.

## Which works better in a small bathroom?

Small bathrooms are where this decision gets most heated, and where the answer is least obvious.

### The case for a wet room

A wet room removes the enclosure and the tray edge. With no door to swing and no step, a compact room feels larger because you can see the whole floor in one sweep. A single floor tile running from the doorway to the shower area makes the room look continuous. In a narrow bathroom, where a standard enclosure would take up the width, a fixed glass panel can free several centimetres.

It also helps when the room is an awkward shape. A tray comes in set sizes. A wet room floor can be any shape the walls make, so a shower can fill a corner or an alcove that no standard tray would fit.

### The case for a shower tray

Small rooms bring a practical problem with wet rooms: water. With no door, spray travels. In a very small room, the toilet, the basin, the towel rail and the back of the door can all be within range of the shower head. A wet room in a tiny space works only when you plan the layout around the water, with the shower head position, the glass panel and the drain all chosen together.

A tray with a door contains the water completely. The rest of the room stays dry, so you can use a wooden vanity unit, a heated towel rail near the shower, or a fabric blind without worrying about them. For a small family bathroom used by several people every morning, that predictability counts for a lot.

### Our view for small rooms

If the room is under about two metres wide and the toilet and basin sit close to the shower, a tray with a good glass door is often the safer choice. If the room is small but has a clear zone for the shower, away from the other fittings, a wet room with a fixed glass panel can make it feel bigger. We decide this by measuring the room, not by following a rule.

## Upkeep and resale

### Cleaning and maintenance

Both options need regular cleaning, but the work is different.

A tray shower has silicone sealant where the tray meets the wall tiles and where the door frame meets the tray. That sealant is the part to watch. Check it every few months, and replace it when it blackens or starts to peel, because water behind a failed seal can reach the floor and wall behind the tray. The glass door needs wiping down to stop limescale building up, particularly in hard water areas. The tray surface itself wipes clean in seconds.

A wet room has more grout. The floor is tiled, and the grout lines are where soap residue and limescale collect. A smaller mosaic tile gives more grip underfoot but also more grout to clean. Larger tiles reduce the grout, but the fall to the drain limits how large a tile you can use. Sealing the grout when it is new helps, and so does squeegeeing the floor after use.

The bigger long-term question for a wet room is the waterproofing. When it is done properly and covered with tiles, you should never see it again. If it fails, the damage is hidden, and finding the leak can mean lifting tiles. This is the main reason to choose your installer carefully and to have the work overseen by someone who knows the waterproofing system.

### Resale value

We do not have data on how either option affects a sale price, and we would be wary of anyone who claims they do. What we can say is how buyers tend to react.

A tray shower is what most people expect to find, so it rarely puts anyone off. A well-built wet room can appeal to buyers who want step-free access, for example older buyers or anyone planning to stay in the house for the long term. A poorly built one, or one with an obvious damp problem, can put buyers off quickly, because they know a failed wet room is costly to put right.

If you are renovating to sell within a few years, a tray shower is the lower-risk choice. If you are staying put, choose what suits your household.

## Our recommendation by situation

Here is how we would steer you, depending on what matters most.

**You have a small family bathroom on an upper floor.** Choose a tray with a glass door. The floor is simpler to build, the water stays where it should, and you avoid structural changes to the joists.

**You have a ground-floor bathroom or a downstairs shower room on a concrete floor.** A wet room is a strong option here. The drain and fall are easier to create, and the cost gap narrows.

**You want step-free access now or in the future.** Choose a wet room, or a low-profile tray set flush into the floor if the structure will not allow a full wet room. Either gives you a level entry.

**Your budget is tight.** Choose a tray. The cost is easier to predict, and the money you save can go towards better tiles or a better door.

**You are selling in the next few years.** Choose a tray, for the reasons above.

**You are converting a bathroom and removing a bath to make space.** This is where a wet room often pays off, because the shower can take over the whole end of the room. We will check the floor first, then confirm the drain position and the fall before we price it.

## How long will the work take?

Bathrooms take two to six weeks on site, depending on size, scope, whether the layout changes and access. A wet room tends to sit towards the longer end of that range, because the waterproofing and floor-forming stages each need to be completed and checked before tiling starts.

Before that, a pre-site phase usually takes one to two weeks. Our logistics team schedules deliveries and removes existing furniture and strip-out waste, so nothing is installed out of order.

The stage order is the same for both options: strip back to brick, first fix plumbing, tiling, then second fix. The main causes of delay are changes to plans, delivery errors, mixed-up drawings and access issues. Deciding between a wet room and a tray before the work starts removes one of the biggest causes of change. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is a wet room cheaper than a shower tray?

Not usually. A wet room removes the cost of the tray and the door, but it adds waterproofing, floor-forming and more tiling labour. On a timber floor, the structural work can add more. A tray shower is typically easier to price in advance. Our itemised quote shows the labour for each stage, so you can compare the two against your own floor.

### Can I have a wet room upstairs?

Yes, but the floor has to be suitable. The waste pipe needs enough fall to drain, and the floor must be rigid enough to carry tiles without flexing. We check the joists and the pipe route at the consultation, before we price anything.

### Do wet rooms leak?

A wet room that is waterproofed properly and tiled over it should not leak. Problems usually come from rushed waterproofing, poorly treated joins around pipes and corners, or a floor that moves. This is why the quality of the installer and the oversight on site matter more than the product.

### Is a wet room harder to clean?

The work differs. You clean more grout, but there is no tray edge or door seal to scrub. Sealing the grout and drying the floor after use keeps it manageable. You can find more practical guidance in our [bathroom resources](/resources/bathroom).

### Which is better for older people?

A level-access shower is easier to enter with a walking aid, and a wet room gives you the most flexibility on layout. A low-profile tray can also work well if the structure does not allow a full wet room. If step-free access matters to you, tell us at the start so we can plan the floor to suit.
