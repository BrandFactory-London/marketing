# Wet room or shower tray: which suits your bathroom?

Excerpt: Wet room vs shower tray: compare cost, installation, small-bathroom fit and upkeep, with a clear recommendation for each type of home.

If you are weighing up a wet room vs shower tray for your next bathroom, the right answer depends on three practical things: the floor you are building on, how much you want to spend, and how much cleaning you are willing to do. Both options give you a good shower. They differ in how the water is contained, how much building work sits underneath the tiles, and how the room behaves day to day. This guide walks through each difference and ends with a recommendation for the most common situations we see in homes across Chigwell, Loughton and Ilford.

## What is a wet room, and what is a shower tray?

### The wet room

A wet room is a bathroom where the shower area sits level with the rest of the floor. There is no tray and usually no step. Instead, the floor is laid to a gentle fall so water runs towards a drain, either a square centre drain or a long linear drain against a wall.

Because water can reach more of the room, the floor and the lower walls are waterproofed before any tiles go down. This is called tanking: a liquid or sheet membrane with sealing tape at every joint and corner. Most wet rooms also have a fixed glass screen to keep spray off the toilet and basin.

On a timber floor, the fall usually comes from a pre-formed wet room board (a former) fitted to the joists. On concrete, it is created in the screed, which may mean breaking out part of the slab to set the drain at the right depth.

### The shower tray

A shower tray is a pre-made base that collects water and sends it to a waste outlet. The tray does the waterproofing job for the shower area, so the rest of the bathroom floor is built and finished in the normal way.

Trays come in several materials. Stone resin is the most common in UK bathrooms because it is rigid and fairly light. Acrylic-capped trays are lighter again and cheaper. Ceramic and solid surface trays sit at the higher end. Low-profile trays, typically around 25mm to 45mm high, give a near-flush look without the floor work of a wet room. Many now come with a textured, slip-resistant finish.

A tray is usually paired with a glass enclosure, a door, or a single fixed panel. The single-panel version is often sold as a "walk-in shower", and it is the closest a tray gets to the open feel of a wet room.

## Cost and installation: wet room vs shower tray

### Where the extra cost of a wet room comes from

A wet room almost always costs more than a tray shower of the same size. The difference sits in the work you never see once the room is finished:

- Tanking the whole floor and the lower walls, rather than only the shower walls.
- Creating the fall, either with a former on timber or new screed on concrete.
- A specialist drain, which costs more than a standard tray waste.
- More floor tiling, because tiles on a fall often need smaller formats or careful cutting so they follow the slope.

A shower tray needs a level, solid base and a waste pipe with enough fall to reach the soil stack. If the waste run is long or the floor is solid concrete, the tray may need to sit on a riser kit or a raised plinth so the pipe has room to fall. That keeps costs down but adds height.

### How each option fits into the build

Our bathrooms follow the same order on site: strip back to brick, first fix plumbing, tiling, then second fix. A wet room adds work to the early stages. The floor is opened up, the drain and former are set to the right levels during first fix, and the tanking has to be applied and fully cured before a single tile goes down. A tray shower moves through first fix faster because the tray is a single unit that is bedded, levelled and connected.

On site, our bathrooms take two to six weeks depending on size, scope, access and whether the layout changes. A wet room in an older house with timber joists will usually sit towards the longer end of that range, especially if the floor needs strengthening or the drain needs to move. A like-for-like tray swap in a modern home tends to sit at the shorter end.

Before work starts, there is a pre-site phase of one to two weeks. Our logistics team schedules deliveries, removes existing furniture and strip-out waste, and handles any structural removal such as a partition wall. For a wet room, the drain, former and tanking kit must all arrive before the floor is opened.

### A note on floors

The floor is the biggest single factor in the wet room vs shower tray decision. Ask these questions early:

- Is the bathroom floor timber or concrete? Timber is usually easier to form a fall in, as long as the joists are sound and there is depth to work with.
- Which way do the joists run? A linear drain works best when it can sit across the joists, not along them.
- Is the bathroom upstairs? A leak from a wet room on a first floor reaches the ceiling below, so the tanking has to be done properly and tested.

## Which works best in a small bathroom?

### The case for a wet room in a small space

In a narrow bathroom or a small en suite, a shower tray and enclosure can eat the room. A 900mm square enclosure with a hinged door needs clearance for the door to swing, and the frame breaks up the floor visually. A wet room removes the tray edge and often the door, so the floor reads as one surface and the room feels larger. You can also use the full width of the room for the shower if the layout allows.

### The case against

In a small room, a wet room makes more of the space a splash zone. Towels, toilet roll and anything on the floor near the shower will get wet unless the screen is well placed. Without a screen, the floor near the toilet can stay damp for some time after each shower. Electrical zones also matter here: a wet room puts more of the room within the areas where lights, fans and shaver sockets must meet stricter rules, which can limit where you put things.

### The middle option

For many small bathrooms, a low-profile tray with a single fixed glass panel gives most of the open look of a wet room at a lower cost. The step is small, there is no door to swing, and the waterproofing is concentrated in one area. If the bathroom is under around two metres wide, we usually suggest pricing both this option and a full wet room before you decide.

## Upkeep and resale

### Cleaning and maintenance

A shower tray is easy to clean. It is one smooth surface, usually with a removable waste cover, and the main job is clearing hair from the trap. The silicone bead where the tray meets the wall needs checking every so often and replacing when it starts to discolour or lift. A failed silicone joint is the most common cause of small leaks around trays, and it is cheap to fix if caught early.

A wet room has more grout on the floor, and floor grout gets walked on and stays wet longer. Choose an epoxy grout or a well-sealed cementitious grout to cut down on staining. The drain needs regular clearing, and linear drains in particular collect hair and soap along the channel, so pick one with a cover you can lift without tools. The upside is that there are no tray edges or enclosure tracks for mould to settle in. Good ventilation is essential in either case, but a wet room depends on it more, because the whole floor gets wet.

### Durability

The long-term risk with a wet room is a failure in the tanking. If the membrane is applied well, it will outlast the tiles. If it is rushed, or not left to cure, water can find its way through grout and into the floor structure without any visible sign for months. This is why who builds a wet room matters more than which drain or tile you choose. With a tray, the risk is lower and easier to spot. A cracked tray or a failed seal usually shows itself quickly, and the tray can be replaced without lifting the whole floor.

### Resale

A well-built wet room can appeal to buyers who want step-free access, including older buyers and anyone planning for reduced mobility later. It can also look good in listing photos.

The main resale caution is about baths. If the wet room replaces the only bath in the house, some buyers, especially families with young children, will see that as a drawback. If you have a second bathroom or an en suite with a bath, that concern largely goes away. A shower tray is a neutral choice for resale: almost no buyer will mark a house down for having one.

## Our recommendation

There is no single winner in the wet room vs shower tray question. Here is how we would advise you in the situations we see most often.

**You have a small or medium family bathroom, and it is the only bathroom in the house.** Keep a bath, and use a shower over the bath or a separate tray shower if space allows. A wet room here can hurt resale and makes the whole room wet for everyone.

**You have a small en suite and want it to feel bigger.** Price a low-profile tray with a fixed glass panel first. If the floor is timber with good depth, also price a wet room. Go for the wet room if the extra cost fits your budget and you are happy to keep on top of floor grout.

**You are planning for accessibility, now or in future.** Choose a wet room. Level access is the main reason most people choose one, and it is much easier to build it in during a full renovation than to retrofit later.

**You are working to a tight budget or a tight timeline.** Choose a shower tray. Less floor work means fewer things that can delay the job, and the cost is easier to predict.

**You have a large bathroom and a second bathroom with a bath.** This is the best case for a wet room. There is room for a long screen, the spray zone stays away from the toilet, and resale is not affected by losing a bath.

**You are on a first floor with a ceiling below that has history of leaks.** Start with a tray, or have the floor and existing pipework assessed fully before considering a wet room.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a wet room more likely to leak than a shower tray?

Not if it is built properly. A wet room relies on tanking under the tiles across the whole floor, so the quality of that work decides how it performs. A tray relies mainly on the silicone seal around its edge. Tray leaks are easier to spot and fix. Wet room leaks are rare when the membrane is applied and cured correctly, but they are harder to trace if they do happen.

### Can I have a wet room on an upstairs floor?

Yes. Many wet rooms are built on first floors with timber joists. The installer needs to check the joist direction, depth and condition, and the tanking must be done carefully because any leak would reach the ceiling below.

### Does a wet room add value to a house?

It can, if it is well built and the house still has a bath elsewhere. Buyers looking for level access often see a wet room as a plus. If it replaces the only bath, some buyers will see it as something to change.

### What is the difference between a walk-in shower and a wet room?

A walk-in shower usually uses a low-profile tray with a single fixed glass panel and no door. A wet room has no tray: the whole floor is waterproofed and slopes to a drain.

### How long does it take to install a wet room?

Our bathrooms take two to six weeks on site, after a pre-site phase of one to two weeks. A wet room tends to sit towards the longer end, because the floor work and tanking add time before tiling can start.
